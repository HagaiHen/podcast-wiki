# Startup Fundraising

**Summary:** Most investor pitches end in "no", and the most frustrating outcome isn't a harsh answer but being strung along without one. A founder who pitched 70–80 times recommends asking each investor to explain a "no" while committing not to argue, and above all running investor conversations in parallel: a deal isn't closed until it's closed, and one fund that strings you along can cost you the round. Exclusivity ("no-shop") demands are a negotiation point, not a given. Meanwhile the cost of starting a software company has fallen from millions (servers, offices) to near zero.

## Key ideas
- Most pitches are rejections; the worst investor behavior is not saying no clearly ("don't pull me along") ([[episodes/tsarot-baheitek--90-naftali-bennett]], [[people/naftali-bennett]]).
- Ask for the reason behind a "no" and commit up front not to say "but you're wrong"; some investors will then explain honestly ([[episodes/tsarot-baheitek--90-naftali-bennett]], [[people/naftali-bennett]]).
- Always fundraise in parallel: an American fund strung him along almost to the end, and not running other conversations was his biggest mistake ([[episodes/tsarot-baheitek--90-naftali-bennett]], [[people/naftali-bennett]]).
- Push back on no-shop clauses: ask why exclusivity is needed and what it's worth ([[episodes/tsarot-baheitek--90-naftali-bennett]], [[people/naftali-bennett]]).
- Starting cost then and now: his first company (an early ASP, later called SaaS, then cloud) needed ~$2M just to start, for servers and offices; today it's close to zero ([[episodes/tsarot-baheitek--90-naftali-bennett]], [[people/naftali-bennett]]).
- Pitch structure he'd use: experienced repeat founder who knows the market and competitors, the best team, a ready business plan, and the hungriest team ([[episodes/tsarot-baheitek--90-naftali-bennett]], [[people/naftali-bennett]]).
