# Startup Fundraising

**Summary:** Most investor pitches end in "no", and the most frustrating outcome isn't a harsh answer but being strung along without one. A founder who pitched 70–80 times recommends asking each investor to explain a "no" while committing not to argue, and above all running investor conversations in parallel: a deal isn't closed until it's closed, and one fund that strings you along can cost you the round. Exclusivity ("no-shop") demands are a negotiation point, not a given. Even a signed term sheet isn't money: SoftBank pulled one a week after Carbyne had told every other fund no. Before product-market fit, investors (often unconventional angels) bet on the founder; only after it do the numbers talk and institutional funds come in. Meanwhile the cost of starting a software company has fallen from millions (servers, offices) to near zero.

## Key ideas
- Most pitches are rejections; the worst investor behavior is not saying no clearly ("don't pull me along") ([[episodes/tsarot-baheitek--90-naftali-bennett]], [[people/naftali-bennett]]).
- Ask for the reason behind a "no" and commit up front not to say "but you're wrong"; some investors will then explain honestly ([[episodes/tsarot-baheitek--90-naftali-bennett]], [[people/naftali-bennett]]).
- Always fundraise in parallel: an American fund strung him along almost to the end, and not running other conversations was his biggest mistake ([[episodes/tsarot-baheitek--90-naftali-bennett]], [[people/naftali-bennett]]).
- Push back on no-shop clauses: ask why exclusivity is needed and what it's worth ([[episodes/tsarot-baheitek--90-naftali-bennett]], [[people/naftali-bennett]]).
- Starting cost then and now: his first company (an early ASP, later called SaaS, then cloud) needed ~$2M just to start, for servers and offices; today it's close to zero ([[episodes/tsarot-baheitek--90-naftali-bennett]], [[people/naftali-bennett]]).
- Pitch structure he'd use: experienced repeat founder who knows the market and competitors, the best team, a ready business plan, and the hungriest team ([[episodes/tsarot-baheitek--90-naftali-bennett]], [[people/naftali-bennett]]).
- Dozens of "no"s before the first yes, and at later stages too ([[episodes/yasmins-living-room--amir-elichai-carbyne]], [[people/amir-elichai]]).
- Reaching an investor cold: he chased Ehud Barak for a long time. After Barak's team said "not interested" he messaged him directly, got a call, then a home meeting that ended with "we're with you". Barak later said he backed a person who would come in through the window if the door was closed ([[episodes/yasmins-living-room--amir-elichai-carbyne]], [[people/amir-elichai]]).
- Interest has to match: Barak was already investing in smart cities, so persistence alone wouldn't have worked otherwise ([[episodes/yasmins-living-room--amir-elichai-carbyne]], [[people/yasmin]]).
- Before product-market fit (about four years for Carbyne) money came from wealthy individuals such as Robert Kraft and David Petraeus, betting on the person. After PMF and the move to the US, Founders Fund and then corporates like Cox came in ([[episodes/yasmins-living-room--amir-elichai-carbyne]], [[people/amir-elichai]]).
- A term sheet isn't a deal: in a COVID-era Series C, SoftBank's term sheet ($250M valuation, $50M secondary) was withdrawn after an internal committee, a week after he had turned down the other four. A board member's fund stepped in with a first and second close and Cox Enterprises joined, closing $56M ([[episodes/yasmins-living-room--amir-elichai-carbyne]], [[people/amir-elichai]]).
- His lesson from that: don't say no to other investors, and be careful who you tell, until the money has moved ([[episodes/yasmins-living-room--amir-elichai-carbyne]], [[people/amir-elichai]]).
